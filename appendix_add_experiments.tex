\chapter{Additional Experiments}
\label{app:ch:additional_experiments}
% \section{WIP --- EDM-CSDI Sampling exploration}
In this chapter, we discuss some of the explorations conducted to assess whether sample improvements are possible and how the model behaves when stochasticity is re-introduced during the generation phase.

 Three sampling explorations were covered: the second-order Heun stochastic sampler (Heun SDE for brevity), the \(\sigma_{\max}\) sampler prior, and an NFEs exploration. Specifically, the first evaluates whether the model can recover some peculiarities of FTS paths through the injection of stochasticity; the second explores whether a prior further from or closer to the original distribution may influence sampling quality while retaining the same computational cost; and the last checks whether the reconstruction error is already under control with the current settings (\(NFEs=400\) or \(steps=200\)), or whether there is margin for improvement or reduction, given that this was an upper bound.
\subsection{Heun Stochastic Sampler}
In the first phase, we generated data under the same conditions as sub-Sec.\ \ref{subsec:conditiona_edm_csdi}, using EDM-CSDI (Cond.) with a stochastic sampler instead. Specifically, we experimented with a budget of \(S_{\text{churn}}=10\), \(S_{\text{tmin}}=0.05\), \(S_{\text{tmax}}=\infty\), and \(S_{\text{noise}}=1.0\). Over \(20,000\) windows, the distributional properties remained largely unchanged.

As shown in Tab.\ \ref{tab:sampler_experiment} and Tab.\ \ref{tab:edm_sampler_distributional_metrics}, the advantage of using SDE is that the injection of noise brings new possible trajectories, increasing the standard deviation. While this is positive, as it brings us closer to the original distribution, it also causes kurtosis to increase and skewness to grow beyond the reference. This is probably due to the stochasticity pushing the model into regions that are seldomly seen, hence poorly learned.

\section{Volatility Regime Analysis}
\label{sec:volatility_regime_analysis}
The experiments in the previous section assess the aggregate, marginal fidelity of the generated paths. A complementary, and arguably stricter, question is whether EDM-CSDI has internalised the \emph{specific market conditions} of distinct historical periods: do windows generated for a calendar span that was historically turbulent exhibit higher volatility than windows generated for a calm one? Answering this probes whether the model exploits the calendar/time side-information embedding (Sec.~\ref{sec:model_architecture}) and the conditioning trajectory, rather than collapsing every window onto a single regime-agnostic average.

\textbf{Setup} \quad For every window a single volatility scalar is computed as the standard deviation of its $512$ Close log-returns (unnormalised, so values correspond to percentage-point return variability). The reference corpus comprises $25{,}226$ overlapping windows; because windows slide with stride $100$, each calendar date is covered by many windows, and a date-indexed signal $\texttt{period\_ref\_std}$ is obtained by averaging the window-std across all windows spanning that date. This signal peaks at known crises (1987, the dot-com bust of 2000--2002, the 2008--2009 GFC, and the 2020 COVID shock; Fig.~\ref{fig:vol_regime_overlay}), confirming the proxy captures real market dynamics. Each generated window is then mapped, via its exact reconstructed dates, to a $\texttt{period\_ref\_std}$ value and classified into a \emph{Low}, \emph{Medium}, or \emph{High} regime using tertile thresholds computed \emph{on the reference corpus only} ($q_{\text{low}}=0.014486$, $q_{\text{high}}=0.020251$), avoiding any leakage from the generated samples into the regime boundaries. The same reference data and EDM noise hyperparameters ($P_{\text{mean}}=-1.4$, $P_{\text{std}}=1.8$) are used throughout; the two settings compared differ only in conditioning mode: \emph{UNCO} (unconditional generation, \texttt{unconditional} mask) and \emph{COND} (\texttt{predict\_close}, conditioned on the real Open/High/Low path).\footnote{In UNCO, $457/20{,}237$ unique windows ($2.3\%$) fall in an ``Unknown'' regime because three source CSVs were not found under the reference root and their dates could not be reconstructed; COND succeeded for all $20{,}239$ windows. This marginally reduces the UNCO regime sample but does not affect the COND results or the qualitative conclusions.}

\textbf{Both models learn a significant regime effect} \quad In both settings the Kruskal--Wallis test rejects the null of equal volatility across regimes with $p\approx0$ (UNCO $H=7407.80$, COND $H=5204.49$), and all three pairwise Mann--Whitney comparisons (Bonferroni-corrected) are significant and in the correct direction (High $>$ Medium $>$ Low). The unconditional model even attains a \emph{larger} effect size ($\eta^2\approx0.374$ vs $0.257$): its regime groups are more cleanly separated relative to their own internal spread. Clean separation, however, is not the same as correct calibration, as the absolute levels reveal.

\textbf{Calibration of the absolute level} \quad Tab.~\ref{tab:vol_regime_calibration} reports, per regime, the mean window-std of the reference corpus against the two models and the corresponding generated-to-reference ratio. UNCO systematically \emph{under-generates} volatility, and the shortfall widens monotonically with the true level, from $-13\%$ in the Low regime to $-26\%$ in the High regime: the classic shrinkage of a model with no information about which window it is producing. COND, by contrast, tracks the reference level to within $1$--$6\%$, with the error essentially vanishing in the High regime ($-1.3\%$). The same picture holds for the regime contrast: the true High/Low ratio is $0.02350/0.01381\approx1.70$, which UNCO compresses to $1.45$ ($\sim15\%$ too flat) while COND reproduces it almost exactly at $1.77$.

\textbf{The correlation ``paradox''} \quad Counter-intuitively, UNCO shows a \emph{higher} linear correlation with the date-based reference signal than COND (OLS $r=0.687$ vs $0.504$, both $p\approx0$). This does not contradict the calibration result. $\texttt{period\_ref\_std}$ is a \emph{cross-sectional}, date-indexed signal: the average volatility across all stocks' windows covering a given calendar span. UNCO has no access to the specific window's OHL path, so its only possible source of date-dependent behaviour is the calendar/time embedding in the side-information; whatever volatility it produces is necessarily a smooth function of date and therefore correlates well with a date-indexed reference, but it is compressed in range. COND additionally observes the realised OHL path of that specific stock, which encodes idiosyncratic volatility that can deviate from the cross-sectional date average; this idiosyncratic signal dominates the generated Close std, yielding far better absolute calibration but adding scatter relative to the smoothed date signal, hence the lower $r$. In short, UNCO produces a smoother but compressed function of calendar time, while COND produces a noisier but correctly-scaled function of the actual conditioning trajectory.

\textbf{Sample-to-sample consistency} \quad Drawing five samples per window exposes how deterministically each model fixes the volatility level. COND's five samples agree on window volatility to within $\sim2\%$ of their mean, roughly constant across regimes, whereas UNCO's samples disagree by $21$--$26\%$, about an order of magnitude larger relative spread (Fig.~\ref{fig:vol_regime_spread}). Conditioning on OHL pins the volatility level almost deterministically; without it, the model treats volatility as a free latent variable. Consistently, window-to-window variability is $2.7$--$3.4\times$ larger for COND, as expected of a model responding to per-window conditioning rather than emitting a regime-level average. Tab.~\ref{tab:vol_regime_summary} collects these diagnostics.

\textbf{Synthesis} \quad Both models have learned \emph{some} notion of period-specific volatility: the regime effect is highly significant ($p\approx0$) and always in the correct direction. The unconditional model's signal is real but heavily shrunk: it relies on a calendar embedding that correlates with the true date-based regime ($r=0.687$) yet compresses the dynamic range by $\sim15\%$, underestimates volatility increasingly with the true level (up to $-26\%$ in the High regime), and cannot reproduce tail volatility (capped at $0.033$ against a historical maximum of $0.108$). The conditional model is dramatically better calibrated in absolute terms, matching regime means within $1$--$6\%$, reproducing the true High/Low contrast, and attaining near-historical-maximum volatility during crisis windows; the price is a lower correlation with the cross-sectional date signal and larger window-to-window variability, both expected consequences of conditioning on the specific OHL trajectory rather than a smoothed calendar average. The model therefore does replicate specific market conditions, but does so chiefly through the conditioning path rather than through the calendar embedding alone.
