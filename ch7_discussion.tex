\section{Discussion of Results}
\label{sec:discussion_results}

Across the experiments reported in Ch.~\ref{ch:experiments_results}, EDM-CSDI (Cond.) consistently outperforms the other models, and several further patterns emerge from a cross-phase reading of the results.

\textbf{Prediction Target and the Zero-return Spike} \quad Across Figs.\ \ref{fig:level_marginal_ve_exponantial_2048} and \ref{fig:density_edm_unconditional}. Phase~1 models predict the injected noise \(\epsilon\), and their generated ECDF cuts straight through the central jump at zero returns. This indicates that the \(\epsilon\)-target does not carry any information about the discreteness of the underlying price grid. EDM-CSDI (Unc.), which predicts \(x_0\) directly, does the opposite and slightly overshoots the same spike (Fig.\ \ref{fig:density_edm_unconditional}). The zero-return spike therefore works as a single statistic check of whether a given target parametrization capture the near-discrete (prices are in cents) hidden in an otherwise continuous log-returns variable.

\textbf{Conditioning, Volatility Regimes, and Two Tail Failure Modes} \quad Every unconditional configuration in this thesis underestimates excess kurtosis: VE+Exponential reaches only 14.3--14.4 against a reference of 36.0--39.5 (Tabs.\ \ref{tab:phase1_aggregate} and \ref{tab:ve_phase1_512_100}), and EDM-CSDI (Unc.) reaches 20.0 against 39.5, a 49\% deficit (Tab.\ \ref{tab:phase2_edm_unconditional_512_100}). Conditioning on same-day OHL closes this deficit (39.0 vs.\ 39.5, Tab.\ \ref{tab:phase2_edm_conditional_512_100}) and improves roughly five times \(W_1\). A possible reading is that unconditional generation is implicitly a mixture problem: the training pool combine calm and turbulent regimes, and a model sampling unconditionally must reproduce both without being told which one is which. Unless it can be inferred from the noised data alone, samples are pulled towards an average regime. Because OHL are strongly correlated with realized volatility (Ch.\ \ref{app:ch:data_insights}), they provide the missing regime indicator.

On the other hand, GARCH-X and Merton overstate kurtosis by two to three orders of magnitude (11021.7 and 3736.4 vs.\ 38.5, Tab.\ \ref{tab:baseline_aggregate}) due to a small fraction of paths escaping their working windows. The two model families fail in opposite ways: parametric models occasionally diverge, while diffusion models trained without conditioning information are pulled toward the mean and underrepresent rare extremes \cite{vincent2011connection,yu2026diffusion}.

\textbf{Sharper but Not Better Calibrated} \quad The conditional model reduces MAE from \(\approx0.62\) (climatology) to \(\approx0.19\) and reaches a CRPS skill score of 0.7376 (Tab.\ \ref{tab:crps_results}), yet remains overconfident at the 95\% and 99\% nominal levels even with 50 samples per window (Tab.\ \ref{tab:coverage_results}). Part of this gap may simply be a finite sample artifact: for an interval built from the min/max of \(N\) i.i.d.\ draws (where \(N=25\) or \(50\) full-length paths sampled per window under the same OHL conditioning each contributing one value per position) there is an expected coverage provided by order statistics of \((N-1)/(N+1)\), i.e.\ \(24/26\approx92.3\%\) for \(N=25\) and \(49/51\approx96.1\%\) for \(N=50\), regardless of how many positions or windows the resulting intervals are averaged over.\footnote{This figure follows from treating the realized outcome as exchangeable with the \(N\) generated values under calibration: each of the \(N+1\) values is equally likely to be the overall minimum or maximum, so the realized outcome lies inside \([\min,\max]\) with probability \(1-2/(N+1)=(N-1)/(N+1)\); under-dispersion relative to the true distribution would push observed coverage below this level.} This is close to the observed 93.46\% and 96.89\% at the 99\% level (Val split), aggregated across all 512 positions of the 2{,}000 evaluated windows. With only 25 and 50 samples per position, a 99\% interval's endpoints fall outside the range of the available order statistics. How much of the remaining gap reflects genuine under-dispersion of the learned density, once this finite-\(N\) effect is accounted for, is left open.

\textbf{Champion Selection and Scale Transfer} \quad The Phase~1 champions carried into Phase~2, VE+Exponential and VE+Cosine, were selected on \(D_{KS}\)/\(W_1\), the metrics on which they are indeed best (0.037/0.002 and 0.062/0.003, Tab.\ \ref{tab:phase1_aggregate}) and also by considering Kim et al.\ \cite{kim2025diffusion} findings. VP+Cosine, however, achieved the tail exponent closest to the reference (\(\hat{\alpha}=4.368\) vs.\ \(4.378\), error \(0.010\), against \(0.083\) for VE+Exponential) but was not retained due to computational and time limitation. VE also offers a direct comparison with EDM, since both build on the VE SDE formulation. It was found that regenerating the VE champions at \(L=512\) produced no systematic improvement over their \(L=2048\) behaviour (Tab.\ \ref{tab:ve_phase1_512_100}): the kurtosis deficit persistsed.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Training, Sampler, and Architecture Ablation} \quad No formal training convergence study was performed (only empirically), and some hyperparameters were fixed at literature defaults rather than tuned for FTS:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(\sigma_{\text{data}}\): fixed to 1 by construction, with its window-wise variation and interaction with \(c_{\text{skip}}\)/\(c_{\text{out}}\) not explored.
    \item \(\sigma_{\max}\) was fixed rather than data-derived (Sec.\ \ref{sec:future_work} discusses an alternative), and \(c_{\text{noise}}(\sigma)\) used the \(1/4\) scaling of Karras et al.\ \cite{karras2022elucidating}.
    \item Sampler: \(\rho=7\) (EDM default) was used throughout, although \(\rho=3\) equalizes truncation error and may suit FTS better given that volatility clustering depends on mid-range \(\sigma\); stochastic churn (\(S_{\text{churn}}>0\)) was not varied and NFEs values were only partially explored.
    \item Architecture: embedding dimensions and fixed sinusoidal positional encoding were inherited from Kim et al.\ \cite{kim2025diffusion} without ablation; learnable positional encodings and skip-connection weights were not tested.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Confounded EDM and Conditioning Effects} \quad The hypothesis that the sharpened zero-return peak of EDM-CSDI (Unc.) follows from \(x_0\)-prediction, rather than some other side effect of the EDM reformulation (Sec.\ \ref{sec:ch6_edm_csdi_phase2}), was not fully isolated experimentally. A \(2\times2\) design (CSDI with \(x_0\)-prediction but without EDM preconditioning, and EDM-CSDI with \(\epsilon\)-prediction) would be needed to differentiate the gains derived from Phase~2 from those related to the EDM reformulation and to OHL conditioning, which were introduced together.

\textbf{Capacity-to-Data Ratio} \quad The conditional model's parameter count (\(\approx3.4\)M, Sec.~\ref{sec:training_config}) is of the same order as the dataset's unique observations (\(\approx2.7\)M, Sec.~\ref{sec:ch4_data}) once the substantial overlap between sliding windows (\(s=100\), \(L=512\)) is accounted for. The strictly held-out validation split shows comparable or better \(D_{KS}\)/\(W_1\) for EDM-CSDI (Cond.) relative to the combined split (Tab.~\ref{tab:phase2_edm_conditional_512_100}), while this raise some concern it could also reflect the informativeness of OHL conditioning rather than genuine generalization.

\textbf{Computational Budget and Scope} \quad The CRPS and coverage analysis (Tabs.\ \ref{tab:crps_results} and \ref{tab:coverage_results}) used 2{,}000 windows, sampling 25--50 paths per window under the same conditioning set due to HPC constraints; as discussed above, the quantity that matters for interval calibration is this \(N\) (N. Samples) per-window and 25--50 remains too few to resolve fully 95--99\% intervals. More broadly, all diffusion models were trained on a single corpus of S\&P~500 close log-returns spanning both decimalization regimes as one undifferentiated dataset, and the only baselines were classical parametric models (GARCH-X, Merton-X). No comparison was made against other deep generative approaches to FTS \cite{yoon2019time,vuletic2024fingan,takahashi2025generation}, so the standing of EDM-CSDI (Cond.) within the broader deep-learning literature remains partly unexplored.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future_work}

\textbf{Random Masking} \quad The \texttt{predict\_close} mask used in Phase~2 is a fixed channel-wise partition, so the model only ever learns \(p(C\mid O,H,L)\). CSDI's \texttt{randmask}, which varies the conditioning set stochastically at each step, would let the model learn conditional distributions over arbitrary channel subsets, for instance predicting the intraday range from the open, or imputing any missing pattern. Whether this generality helps or hurts \texttt{predict\_close} specifically remains open.

\textbf{Principled \(\sigma_{\max}\) Selection and Heavy-Tailed Noising} \quad Rather than abalting \(\sigma_{\max}\) manually, Pandey et al.\ \cite{pandey2024heavytailed}, propose to estimate \(\sigma_{\max}\) by minimizing the mutual information between clean and maximally-noised data, which could remove this hyperparameter entirely. More broadly, \(t\)-EDM, replace the Gaussian noising distribution with a Student-\(t\), preserving the EDM preconditioning structure while changing the tail behaviour of the forward process, directly targeting the heavy tails modeling difficulties discussed in Sec.\ \ref{sec:discussion_results}.

% \textbf{Blended Prediction Targets for the Decimalization Spike} \quad Since \(\epsilon\)-prediction undershoots and \(x_0\)-prediction overshoots the zero-return spike (Sec.\ \ref{sec:discussion_results}), a prediction target that interpolates between the two as a function of \(\sigma\) could be tuned to match the spike's empirical magnitude while retaining EDM's preconditioning. The spike's amplitude offers a cheap target for tuning such a blend.

\textbf{Stochastic Sampling and Constraint-Preserving Conditioning} \quad This thesis used the deterministic Heun ODE sampler (\(S_{\text{churn}}=0\)) for the results of Sec.\ \ref{subsec:conditiona_edm_csdi}. A preliminary stochastic-churn run (Heun SDE, \(S_{\text{churn}}=10\), App.\ \ref{app:ch:additional_experiments}, Tabs.\ \ref{tab:sampler_experiment}, \ref{tab:edm_sampler_distributional_metrics}) cause the standard deviation, tail quantiles, and \(W_1\) to move closer to the reference, partially addressing the heavy tail modeling problem of Sec.\ \ref{sec:discussion_results}, but overshoots kurtosis and skewness, as noise injection pushes sampling into poorly-learned regions. A systematic exploration of \(S_{\text{churn}}\), \(S_{\text{tmin}}\), and \(S_{\text{tmax}}\) is needed to find a setting that improves tail coverage without causing overshooting.

\textbf{Constraint-Preserving Conditioning} \quad Enforcing (H\geq C\geq L) in generated samples would remove a set of structurally invalid paths, but such hard constraint could also restrict the diffusion process's freedom to explore and generate actually novel trajectories, particularly near the edges of the H-L range. Nevertheless, the strength of such a constraint could be varied from a soft penalty to a strict reparameterization, which by itself would be a design choice that affect sample diversity. This remains untested.

\textbf{Financial Metrics} \quad The evaluation in this thesis stayed at the level of distributional and stylized-fact metrics (\(D_{KS}\), \(W_1\), kurtosis, tail exponent, CRPS, coverage), which measure how faithfully the generated log-returns reproduce the reference law but say little about the downstream financial decisions such generated FTS would inform. A natural next step is to assess EDM-CSDI (Cond.) through risk measures that practitioners actually report, most directly Value at Risk (VaR) and Expected Shortfall (ES) at the usual 95\% and 99\% levels. This would ground the generator in a concrete risk-management use case and offer a decision-relevant complement to the purely statistical metrics used here.

\textbf{Regime-Stratified Training, Evaluation, and Reweighting} \quad The divergence in the intensity of some stylized facts between the \emph{Validation} and \emph{Both} splits, and across models, calls for further investigation and generalization, specifically, testing this thesis' findings under other regimes, such as the pre-/post-2002 decimalization process or different realized-volatility regimes' levels. This would clarify whether accuracy is uniform across regimes or concentrated in those dominating the training corpus. If tail erosion is per se partly a consequence of rare windows contributing little to the average training loss, reweighting the training distribution toward these higher-volatility windows, as in class-balancing diffusion \cite{qin2023classbalancing}, could offer a way to test this hypothesis.
