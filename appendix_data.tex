\subsection{Decimalization}
In this section it will be discussed more in depth the decimalization process briefly mentioned in  Sec.\ \ref{sec:ch4_data}, specifically what it is and how it is distributed in our model.

\textbf{Introduction} \quad Decimalization process happen in was mandated by the Security and Exchange Commission in January 2000 and completed by the 9th of April 2001 \cite{furfine2003decimalization}. Before this period US stock prices were quoted in fractions, specifically 1/16th of a dollar, meaning that the minimum price movement needed to record an effective
change in price was 0.0625 dollars. After decimalization this changed to 1 cent.

\textbf{Characteristics} \quad About 7.1928\% of all our returns are exactly 0.0. The distribution of these zero-returns is not equal, specifically they are mostly concentrated around 1980s and 1990s as it is possible to see from the Fig.\ \ref{fig:zero_returns_distribution}.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{images/ch4/zero_returns_distribution_cropped.png}
    \caption{Frequency count of absolute zero returns grouped by year.}
    \label{fig:zero_returns_distribution}
\end{figure}

The maximum recorded was in 1984 with \(10,600\), amounting to 21.374\% of the yearly observations. Interestingly, 0\% returns drop before 1980, this has to due with the fact that with a 40 years historical minimum all the first have data till April 1986, nevertheless as we proceed further back in time, the number of observations fall. Indeed, we move from having 210 tickers till 1986 to have only 91 in the 1970s and 26 in the 1960s.

\textbf{Decimalization Consequences} \quad Decimalization is the cause of the peculiar distribution that provide the peculiar \(q_{0.02}-q_{0.98}\) range shape discussed in the experimental chapter (Ch.\ \ref{ch:experiments_results}). This is even more evident when we look at the evolution of the log-returns distribution in Fig.\ \ref{fig:log_returns_history}.
