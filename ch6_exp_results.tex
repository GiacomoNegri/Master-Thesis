\chapter{Experiments and Results}
\label{ch:experiments_results}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:exp_introduction}

This chapter presents the experimental evaluation of the two modelling phases introduced
in Chapters~\ref{ch:theoretical_framework} and~\ref{ch:data_setup}, assessed with the
metrics defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation_methodology}. Two research questions guide
the analysis:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Phase~1 --- Replication.} Is a CSDI-based diffusion architecture capable
    of learning and reproducing the stylized facts of financial time series when trained
    under the SDE framework proposed by Kim et al.~\cite{kim2025diffusion}?
    \item \textbf{Phase~2 --- EDM-CSDI.} Does embedding the same architecture within the EDM framework of Karras et al.~\cite{karras2022elucidating}
    and conditioning generation on same-day Open, High, and Low log-returns improve
    generation quality over the Phase~1 baseline?
\end{enumerate}

The chapter is organised in two sequential parts that mirror the experimental structure
introduced in Chapter~\ref{ch:theoretical_framework}. Phase~1
(Sec.~\ref{sec:phase1}) replicates the work of Kim et al.~\cite{kim2025diffusion} on
unconditional univariate generation of S\&P~500 close log-returns.
Phase~2 (Sec.~\ref{sec:phase2}) extends the validated backbone to the multivariate OHLC
setting within the EDM framework, evaluating conditional generation quality on both
training windows and fully held-out validation windows.

A key design difference between the two phases concerns the validation split, as described in Sec.\ref{sec:sliding_window_ch4}. In Phase~1,
validation windows are drawn randomly from the full sliding-window pool. In Phase~2, validation windows are strictly held out.

Both phases use the same underlying CSDI-inspired backbone, whose details are available Tab.~\ref{tab:comparisons}
and Tab.~\ref{tab:training_setup}. Metric definitions are readable at Ch. ~\ref{ch:evaluation_methodology}.

\section{Phase 1 --- Replication}
\label{sec:phase1}

\subsection{Setup and Motivation}
\label{sec:phase1_setup}

Phase~1 aims to replicate the generative approach of Kim et al.~\cite{kim2025diffusion},
who applied a CSDI backbone~\cite{tashiro2021csdi} to unconditional generation of
financial time series under three SDE-inspired forward processes. As discussed in
Sec.~\ref{sec:scorediffmodels}, the VE, VP, and GBM-inspired formulations differ in how
variance evolves along the forward diffusion trajectory, and each admits multiple noise
schedule parametrisations. The experimental grid therefore covers three SDE variants
crossed with three noise schedules --- linear, exponential, and cosine --- yielding nine
candidate configurations in principle. Two of these, VP with exponential schedule and VP
with cosine schedule, were not pursued due to time constraints and preliminary evidence
of unpromising training dynamics observed in early runs. The remaining seven
configurations were each trained to convergence and evaluated in full. This grid is
intended to reproduce the main claim of Kim et al.~\cite{kim2025diffusion} that the
GBM-inspired SDE captures the stylized facts of financial returns more faithfully than VE
and VP variants.

All Phase~1 models are trained on univariate close log-returns ($K=1$) using the
unconditional masking strategy, in which $m^{\text{co}} = \mathbf{0}$ everywhere and the
loss is computed on all sequence entries without any side information. Sliding windows of
length $L=2048$ (approximately ten years of daily data) are constructed with stride
$s=400$, yielding approximately 5,500 training windows across the full S\&P~500
constituent dataset. The sampler used for generation is the probability flow ODE, which
produces deterministic trajectories governed entirely by the learned score field and
corresponds to the primary evaluation sampler of Song et al.~\cite{song2021score}.
Remaining architectural and optimiser details are given in Tab.~\ref{tab:training_setup}.

It is important to recall that the stride of $s=400$ is substantially shorter than the
window length $L=2048$, so consecutive windows drawn from the same ticker share
approximately 1,648 time steps. Validation windows drawn at random from the full
window pool will therefore, in most cases, overlap temporally with one or more training
windows for the same ticker. Any measured performance gap between the training and
validation splits should be interpreted with this limitation in mind. This is a known
characteristic of the Phase~1 setup, acknowledged in Sec.~\ref{sec:sliding_window_ch4},
and it provides one motivation for the stricter separation strategy adopted in Phase~2.

\subsection{Aggregate Statistics and Distributional Similarity}
\label{sec:phase1_aggregate}

The first layer of evaluation targets the unconditional marginal distribution of generated
close log-returns. For each of the seven trained configurations, an ensemble of windows
of length $L=2048$ is generated, and the pooled generated returns are compared against
the pooled training returns using the KS statistic $D_{\text{KS}}$ and the Wasserstein
distance $W_1$, as defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation_methodology}. Pathwise
higher-order moments --- mean, variance, skewness, and excess kurtosis --- are computed
window-by-window and then aggregated, providing direct evidence of whether the model
reproduces the non-Gaussian shape of the return distribution beyond the first two
moments. Skewness and excess kurtosis are of particular interest: the former captures
the asymmetry between positive and negative returns, and the latter indicates whether
the model reproduces the heavy-tailed character of the data, which is the defining
feature of financial log-return distributions.

\subsection{Stylized Facts}
\label{sec:phase1_stylized}

Beyond the marginal distribution, a model trained on financial time series should
reproduce the dynamic properties of the data --- specifically the three stylized facts
introduced in Sec.~\ref{sec:fts_literature}: heavy-tailed return distributions,
volatility clustering, and the leverage effect. These properties are evaluated following
the implementation of Takahashi et al.~\cite{stakahashy_fingan}, as described in
Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation_methodology}, consistent with the evaluation protocol of
Kim et al.~\cite{kim2025diffusion} and enabling direct comparison with the figures
reported in that work.

\textbf{Heavy-Tailed Distributions.}
The tail behaviour of the generated return distribution is assessed through log-log tail
distribution plots and the power-law tail exponent $\alpha$, estimated using the
\texttt{powerlaw} package of Alstott et al.~\cite{alstott2014powerlaw}. Recall from
Sec.~\ref{sec:fts_literature} that empirical tail exponents for equity returns typically
fall in the range $\alpha \in [3,5]$, with the S\&P~500 constituents used in this study
yielding an empirical benchmark of approximately $\alpha \approx 4.35$.
% [PLACEHOLDER: confirm empirical alpha value against computed data]
An estimated $\alpha$ below this benchmark indicates that the generated distribution has
heavier tails than the training data, producing too many extreme events; an $\alpha$
above it indicates lighter tails, corresponding to an underestimation of the frequency
of large return shocks. Both deviations carry practical consequences for downstream
applications such as risk management and stress testing.

\textbf{Volatility Clustering.}
Volatility clustering is assessed via the autocorrelation function (ACF) of absolute
returns $|r_t|$, plotted on a log-log scale. As established in
Sec.~\ref{sec:fts_literature}, the ACF of absolute returns in real equity data decays
hyperbolically as $\text{Corr}(|r_t|, |r_{t+k}|) \sim k^{-\beta}$, with $\beta \in
[0.1, 0.5]$, corresponding to a straight line on a log-log plot. A model that fails to
capture this property will exhibit ACF decay that is either exponentially fast,
producing a curve that drops below the empirical reference at short lags, or
effectively flat at zero beyond a small lag horizon. Capturing volatility clustering is
non-trivial for a diffusion model trained on return sequences: it requires the temporal
Transformer to learn and encode persistent amplitude dependencies across hundreds of
time steps within each window of length $L=2048$.

\textbf{Leverage Effect.}
The leverage effect captures the empirical observation that negative return shocks are
followed by disproportionately higher future volatility, and is measured through the
lead-lag correlation $L(k) = \mathbb{E}[r_t r_{t+k}^2] / \mathbb{E}[r_t^2]^2$, which
is persistently negative for small positive lags $k$ in real financial data, as defined
in Sec.~\ref{sec:fts_literature}. Reproducing this property requires the model to
generate sequences in which periods of elevated volatility are systematically associated
with preceding negative returns --- an asymmetric temporal dependency that is absent
from symmetric noise processes unless it is explicitly encoded by the data-driven
component of the model. As discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:scorediffmodels}, the
GBM-inspired forward process introduces a form of heteroskedastic noise structure in
log-price space that may predispose it to capture this asymmetry more readily than the
symmetric VE and VP formulations.

\subsection{Sample Paths}
\label{sec:phase1_paths}

As a qualitative complement to the quantitative metrics, unnormalised sample paths are
examined to assess whether the model generates sequences that resemble plausible equity
price trajectories. The unnormalisation procedure inverts the global z-score applied
during preprocessing, recovering approximate price-level paths that can be visually
compared against historical S\&P~500 price trajectories over a comparable time horizon.
This assessment does not constitute a formal metric; rather, it serves as a plausibility
check. A model may achieve competitive KS and tail exponent values while still generating
paths with structurally unrealistic features, such as monotonically trending returns,
implausibly persistent volatility regimes, or abrupt discontinuities at window boundaries.
Conversely, a model that produces visually convincing paths but fails on quantitative
metrics suggests that the evaluation metrics may not be capturing the full range of
relevant properties.

\section{Phase 2 --- EDM-CSDI Conditional Generation}
\label{sec:phase2}

\subsection{Setup and Motivation}
\label{sec:phase2_setup}

Phase~2 extends the validated CSDI-backbone architecture to the multivariate conditional
setting within the EDM framework. Relative to Phase~1, three structural changes are
introduced, each with a distinct theoretical motivation.

\textbf{EDM training framework.} The shift from the Song et al.~\cite{song2021score}
SDE formulation to EDM~\cite{karras2022elucidating} replaces the heuristic loss
weighting $\lambda(\sigma_i) = \sigma_i^2$ with the principled scheme
$\lambda(\sigma) = c_{\text{out}}(\sigma)^{-2}$, which equalises the loss contribution
across all noise levels rather than allowing high-noise training steps to dominate the
gradient signal (Sec.~\ref{sec:edm_literature}). EDM also introduces the preconditioning
scheme $D_\theta(x;\sigma) = c_{\text{skip}}(\sigma)x + c_{\text{out}}(\sigma)
F_\theta(c_{\text{in}}(\sigma)x;\,c_{\text{noise}}(\sigma))$ with
$\sigma_{\text{data}} = 1.0$ set by the per-channel z-score normalisation, and replaces
the discrete step index embedding with the continuous noise-level encoding
$c_{\text{noise}}(\sigma) = \frac{1}{4}\ln\sigma$. The noise level at training time is
sampled from a log-normal distribution, $\ln\sigma \sim \mathcal{N}(P_{\text{mean}},
P_{\text{std}}^2)$, concentrating training effort on the noise range that is most
informative for the data at hand. The values $P_{\text{mean}} = -1.4$ and $P_{\text{std}}$
are determined by the ablation described in Sec.~\ref{sec:phase2_ablation}.

\textbf{Multivariate OHLC conditioning.} The model now operates on $K=4$ channels
(Open, High, Low, Close), all expressed as log-returns relative to the previous closing
price as detailed in Sec.~\ref{sec:sliding_window_ch4}. The \texttt{predict\_close}
masking strategy assigns $m^{\text{co}}_{k,l} = 1$ for $k \in \{O, H, L\}$ and
$m^{\text{ta}}_{k,l} = 1$ for $k = C$, so that the Open, High, and Low channels
are fully observed at all time steps and the Close channel is the sole generation target.
This configuration is financially motivated: the intraday range $\hat{H}_t - \hat{L}_t$
is a well-established realised volatility proxy~\cite{parkinson1980extreme}, and the
overnight log-return $\hat{O}_t = \log(O_t / C_{t-1})$ encodes the opening gap with
respect to the previous session. Both signals carry substantive intraday information about
the Close log-return that is unavailable to an unconditional model, as discussed in
Sec.~\ref{sec:ohlc_theoretical}. The central question of Phase~2 is whether the model
learns to exploit these signals to produce conditional forecasts that are both more
accurate and better calibrated than the unconditional Phase~1 baseline.

\textbf{Validation and sampling.} The window length is reduced to $L=512$ (approximately
two years of daily data) and the stride to $s=100$, as necessitated by hardware
constraints (Sec.~\ref{sec:sliding_window_ch4}). Validation windows are strictly held
out: for any ticker contributing a validation window, no training window covers an
overlapping time range for that ticker. At inference, the Heun second-order
predictor--corrector sampler replaces the probability flow ODE used in Phase~1. The Heun
sampler requires two network evaluations per denoising step but reduces the local
truncation error to $\mathcal{O}(h^3)$ and is fully decoupled from the training
objective, meaning the same trained checkpoint can be evaluated with alternative samplers
without retraining (Sec.~\ref{sec:model_architecture}).

The model achieving the best performance in the ablation of Sec.~\ref{sec:phase2_ablation}
is referred to as the Best Model (BM) throughout the remainder of this chapter. All
Phase~2 evaluations reported in subsequent sections use BM unless otherwise stated.

\subsection{Noise Schedule Ablation --- Selecting \texorpdfstring{\(P_{\text{std}}\)}{P\_std}}
\label{sec:phase2_ablation}

The parameter $P_{\text{std}}$ controls the width of the log-normal distribution from
which noise levels $\sigma$ are drawn during training (Eq.~\ref{eq:edm_sigma_sampling}).
A narrow $P_{\text{std}}$ concentrates gradient signal on a limited noise range, which
can improve performance at the dominant noise levels at the cost of reduced coverage of
the extremes. A wide $P_{\text{std}}$ spreads gradient signal across a broader range of
noise levels, potentially improving the model's ability to handle both fine-grained
reconstruction at low $\sigma$ and coarse-scale generation at high $\sigma$, but risks
diluting the gradient at the most informative noise levels. The optimal value is a
calibration question that depends on the distributional properties of the training data
and cannot be derived from first principles.

The ablation is conducted by training the full Phase~2 architecture to convergence
independently for each value of $P_{\text{std}}$ in the grid
$\{1.0, 1.2, 1.4, 1.6, 1.8\}$, with $P_{\text{mean}} = -1.4$ fixed. This value of
$P_{\text{mean}}$ was identified as the best-performing choice across the prior
ablation grid $P_{\text{mean}} \in \{-0.8, -1.0, -1.2, -1.4, -1.6\}$; the full results
of that grid are available in Appendix~\ref{app:sec:training_config}. Models are compared
on validation loss and on the primary generation metrics at a fixed evaluation checkpoint.
The validation loss is the primary selection criterion; KS, $W_1$, and CRPS computed on
training windows serve as supporting evidence that improvements in loss translate to
improvements in generation quality.

\subsection{Conditional Generation Quality}
\label{sec:phase2_conditional}

The defining evaluation of Phase~2 is the quality of the conditional predictive
distribution $p(r^C \mid r^O, r^H, r^L)$ generated by the model at each time step
within each window. Unlike the distributional metrics of Sec.~\ref{sec:phase2_comparison},
which assess whether the marginal distribution of generated Close log-returns resembles
the training data, the CRPS and interval coverage metrics assess whether individual
conditional forecasts are accurate and calibrated given the specific OHL context
provided at inference time.

For each window, the model generates an ensemble of Close log-return trajectories,
each conditioned on the same observed OHL channels. The ensemble is used to compute
the CRPS (as defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation_methodology}) and the empirical

\subsection{Stylized Facts under Conditional Generation}
\label{sec:phase2_stylized}

A conditional model that achieves strong pointwise CRPS performance may still fail to
reproduce the dynamic statistical properties of the Close log-return series at the
sequence level. This section repeats the stylized fact evaluation of
Sec.~\ref{sec:phase1_stylized} on the generated Close channel from Phase~2 BM, using
the same metrics and the same visual format, so that direct comparison with Phase~1 is
possible without any ambiguity about evaluation protocol. In Phase~2, only the Close
channel is generated; the OHL channels retain their observed values and are not evaluated
as generative outputs. The stylized facts are therefore computed on the marginal
distribution of generated Close log-returns within each window.

Conditioning on OHL should not, in principle, prevent the model from reproducing the
stylized facts of the Close marginal: fat tails, volatility clustering, and the leverage
effect are properties of the underlying data-generating process and are present in the
conditional distribution $p(r^C \mid r^O, r^H, r^L)$ as well as in the unconditional
marginal. However, if the model exploits the OHL conditioning to reduce forecast variance
--- for example, by placing most ensemble mass near the conditional mean --- extreme Close
returns may be suppressed relative to their unconditional frequency. If this occurs, it
constitutes an informative result about the trade-off between conditional accuracy and
marginal distributional fidelity under the current model parametrisation.

\subsection{Unconditional EDM Baseline}
\label{sec:phase2_unconditional}

The comparison between Phase~1$^*$ and Phase~2 BM in Sec.~\ref{sec:phase2_comparison}
conflates two distinct sources of any observed performance difference: the change in
training framework (from the Song et al.~\cite{song2021score} SDE formulation to EDM)
and the introduction of OHLC conditioning information. Without an intermediate model
that applies the EDM framework while withholding the conditioning signal, it is not
possible to attribute any observed improvement to conditioning alone rather than to the
framework change.

To isolate these contributions, an unconditional EDM model is trained using the same
architecture, EDM loss, preconditioning, and noise schedule as Phase~2 BM, but with
$m^{\text{co}} = \mathbf{0}$ everywhere and $K=1$, so that only the univariate Close
log-return channel is provided and no OHL side information is available at any stage of
training or inference. This model is structurally identical to Phase~2 BM in its training
framework and window configuration ($L=512$, $s=100$), and structurally identical to
Phase~1$^*$ in its conditioning (none). Any performance difference between Phase~1$^*$
and the unconditional EDM model can therefore be attributed to the framework change,
while any remaining gap between the unconditional EDM model and Phase~2 BM can be
attributed to the OHL conditioning signal. If the unconditional EDM model matches or
exceeds Phase~2 BM on distributional metrics, this would indicate that the EDM framework
itself is responsible for the bulk of any improvement over Phase~1$^*$, and that the OHL
conditioning contributes primarily to conditional forecast calibration rather than to
marginal distributional fidelity.

\subsection{Sampler Ablations}
\label{sec:phase2_sampler}

A key advantage of the EDM framework is the decoupling of the numerical sampler from
the training objective, enabling inference-only experiments without any retraining. The
following three ablations are applied to the Phase~2 BM checkpoint and, where indicated,
to the Phase~1$^*$ checkpoint, to characterise the sensitivity of generation quality to
sampler configuration. All ablations are conducted after training is complete and involve
only changes to the inference procedure.

\textbf{Stochastic Sampler --- \(S_{\text{churn}}\).}
In its deterministic form, the Heun probability flow ODE produces identical Close
trajectories for identical OHL context across all ensemble members, since the ODE is
fully determined by its initial condition and the learned score field. For conditional
generation, this implies that all ensemble samples collapse to the same trajectory for
a given OHL window, eliminating the ability to quantify predictive uncertainty through
ensemble spread. While this is acceptable for point forecasting, it renders the model
unable to represent the distribution $p(r^C \mid r^O, r^H, r^L)$, which is the primary
evaluation object in Phase~2. The parameter $S_{\text{churn}}$ injects controlled
stochastic perturbations at each denoising step, restoring ensemble diversity by adding
noise at a rate calibrated to the local curvature of the score field. Larger values of
$S_{\text{churn}}$ increase the spread of the generated ensemble but may also introduce
additional discretisation error if the perturbations push trajectories into regions of
the score field where the ODE approximation is less accurate.

\textbf{Prior Sensitivity --- \(\sigma_{\max}\).}
The value of $\sigma_{\max}$ at inference time defines the noise level of the prior
distribution from which sampling begins. A value that is too low means the sampler
starts in a partially denoised state, potentially inheriting residual structure from
the initialisation rather than generating from the correct prior. A value that is too
high wastes denoising budget on noise levels that carry no recoverable information about
the data, and may introduce numerical issues if the score estimate is poorly conditioned
at extreme noise levels. This ablation characterises the sensitivity of Phase~1$^*$ and
Phase~2 BM to the choice of $\sigma_{\max}$, providing practical guidance on the
robustness of the inference procedure.

\textbf{Number of Denoising Steps --- NFEs.}
Karras et al.~\cite{karras2022elucidating} report that the Heun sampler achieves sample
quality comparable to Euler--Maruyama at approximately seven times fewer neural function
evaluations, due to the higher-order truncation error and the concentration of
discretisation steps at low $\sigma$ where ODE curvature is highest
(Sec.~\ref{sec:edm_literature}). For deployment in applications such as scenario
generation or Monte Carlo simulation, sampling efficiency is practically relevant, since
the cost of generation scales linearly with the number of denoising steps. In the Heun
sampler, each denoising step requires two network evaluations (one for the predictor and
one for the corrector), so a schedule of $N$ steps incurs a total of $2N$ NFEs.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:experiments_summary}

This chapter has presented the experimental evaluation of two complementary modelling
phases. Phase~1 assessed seven SDE and noise schedule configurations on unconditional
univariate generation of S\&P~500 close log-returns, seeking to replicate the claims of
Kim et al.~\cite{kim2025diffusion}. Phase~2 embedded the same architecture within the
EDM framework with OHLC conditioning, evaluating both conditional forecast calibration
and marginal distributional fidelity on training and strictly held-out validation windows.
