\chapter{Experiments and Results}
\label{ch:experiments_results}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:exp_introduction}
This chapter presents the experimental evaluation of the two modeling phases introduced
in Ch.~\ref{ch:theoretical_framework} and~\ref{ch:data_setup}, assessed with the
metrics defined in Ch.~\ref{ch:evaluation_methodology}. Two research questions guide
the analysis:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Phase~1 --- Replication.} Is a CSDI-based diffusion architecture capable
    of learning the distribution and reproducing the stylized facts of financial time series when trained
    under the SDE framework proposed by Kim et al.~\cite{kim2025diffusion}?
    \item \textbf{Phase~2 --- EDM-CSDI.} Does embedding the same architecture within the EDM framework of Karras et al.~\cite{karras2022elucidating}
    and conditioning generation on same-day Open, High, and Low log-returns improve
    generation quality over the Phase~1 baseline?
\end{enumerate}

The chapter is organised in four sequential parts. The first part introduces two baseline models, GARCH-X and Merton-X, which serve as a baseline comparison against parametric models that are widely accepted in industry, fully understood, and probabilistically modeled. The second part covers Phase~1, which concerns the replication of the work of Kim et al.~\cite{kim2025diffusion} on unconditional univariate generation of S\&P~500 close log-returns. The third part investigates the contribution of the EDM framework when applied to Kim et al.'s CSDI-based architecture, retaining unconditional information while introducing preconditioning in training. Lastly, the full EDM model is presented, incorporating conditional information within the EDM framework.

Subsequently, other, smaller experiments were carried out to compare the models' behavior under different sampling procedures.

A key design difference that is worth keeping in mind between Phase~1 and Phase~2 concerns the validation split, as described in Sec.\ref{sec:sliding_window_ch4}. In Phase~1, validation windows are drawn randomly from the full sliding-window pool and are partially overlapping with others. In Phase~2, validation windows are strictly held out: 239 windows, corresponding to approximately 1.5\% of the overall windows and 10\% of the unique rows.

Moreover, because of the way the data are processed to build windows, the `Reference' values vary slightly according to the specific configuration being analyzed: raw reference, \(L=2048\) windows with \texttt{stride}\(=100\), or \(L=512\) windows with \texttt{stride}\(=100\).

Unless stated otherwise, all power-law coefficients \(\hat{\alpha}\) are computed using the \texttt{powerlaw} Python package by Alstott et al.~\cite{alstott2014powerlaw} on a subsample of \(300,000\) observations. Similarly, \(D_{KS}\) and \(W_1\) are computed on a subsample of 1M observations randomly selected using a fixed seed. Finally, excess kurtosis (Kur.) is reported relative to the Gaussian.

\section{Phase 1: Replication}
\label{sec:phase1_results}
Phase~1 aims to replicate the generative approach of Kim et al.~\cite{kim2025diffusion} as detailed in the first column of Tab.\ \ref{tab:training_setup}. For each model, a dataset of 512 windows of length \(L=2048\) (\(1,048,576\) observations) was generated using the probability-flow ODE (Eq.\ \ref{eq:probability_flow}) with 2000 discretization steps. The number of samples was chosen for computational feasibility.

The statistics above show consistency in performance and have the advantage of being bounded in kurtosis and skewness. The other statistics, as can also be appreciated from Tab.\ \ref{tab:phase1_raw_with_reference}, match the aggregate distributional statistics fairly well; nevertheless, they still fail to learn the specific pattern observable in the \(q_{0.02}-q_{0.98}\) range. The `Kur.' of `VP + Linear' is likely attributable to a poorly learned score field for a few samples and to ODE truncation error.

Importantly, interpreting the log-returns as cumulative did not yield any useful advantage; instead, the learning appeared rudimentary, and the generated values are very close to the forward-added Gaussian noise, casting doubt on the model's ability to discern signal from noise.

    \label{fig:level_marginal_ve_exponantial_2048}
\end{figure}

Clearly the VE SDE is superior to VP with a linear noise scheduler, which smooths out the distribution. This is interesting because, although VP reported lower training losses for the predicted noise \(\hat{\epsilon}\) --- seemingly more promising during training --- it has more difficulty reconstructing the series and is either unstable or oversmooths the distribution, implying that model expressivity might matter more than detailed reconstruction. There are still two distributional limitations of the VE approach: first, it undershoots tail reconstruction, as visible in the QQ-plot of Fig.\ \ref{fig:level_marginal_ve_exponantial_2048}. Secondly, it misses the 0\% returns, as shown by the generated ECDF cutting through the central vertical jump.

\textbf{Stylized Facts} \quad Our estimate of the \(\alpha\) component is consistent with Kim et al., who reported \(\alpha\approx4.35\), while for us it was estimated at \(4.37\). An estimate below this benchmark indicates heavier tails in the data.

The core advantage of Phase~1 models over the baselines is their ability to recover the stylized facts of the training windows, especially the leverage effect, even if at a lower scale than the reference.

\section{Phase 2: EDM-CSDI}

The first part evaluates unconditional generation, with training parameters as defined in Tab.\ \ref{tab:training_setup} (Phase~2 column), but using the \texttt{unconditional} mask instead of the \texttt{predict\_close} one and with the number of features \(K=1\). For a fair comparison with Phase~1, the two most promising models --- VE+Exponential and VE+Cosine --- were retrained with the same settings as in Phase~1 (Tab.\ \ref{tab:training_setup}), but with \(L=512\) and \(s=100\).
The second part explores conditional generation.

\subsection{Phase~2 Unconditional EDM-CSDI}

The next phase focused on understanding how the EDM formulation impacts the generalization and the learning of stylized facts of the CSDI model. From here onward we will refer to the current model as EDM-CSDI (Unc.).

In particular, an improvement was measured in the ability to capture the decimalization 0\% returns effect. This is likely because, in Phase~1, the model still tries to predict the added noise \(\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)\), which makes precise inference of the missed amount harder, whereas the EDM framework, which predicts the output values directly, makes this easier --- possibly overestimating, since it is an easy way to reduce error. Regardless of this improvement, the ability to capture heavy tails seems to remain somewhat limited.

\section{Phase 3 --- EDM-CSDI Sampling exploration}
In this section, we discuss some of the explorations conducted to assess whether sample improvements are possible and how the model behaves when stochasticity is re-introduced during the generation phase.

This section covers three sampling explorations: the second-order Heun stochastic sampler, the \(\sigma_{\max}\) sampler prior, and an NFEs exploration. Specifically, the first evaluates whether the model can recover some peculiarities of FTS paths through the injection of stochasticity; the second explores whether a prior further from or closer to the original distribution may influence sampling quality while retaining the same computational cost; and the last checks whether the reconstruction error is already under control with the current settings (\(NFEs=400\) or \(steps=200\)), or whether there is margin for improvement or reduction, given that this was an upper bound.

\textbf{Heun Stochastic Sampler} \quad In the first phase, we generated data under the same conditions as sub-Sec.\ \ref{subsec:conditiona_edm_csdi}, using EDM-CSDI (Cond.) with a stochastic sampler instead.
