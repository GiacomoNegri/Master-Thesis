\chapter{Experiments and Results}
\label{ch:experiments_results}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:exp_introduction}
This chapter presents the experimental evaluation of the two modeling phases introduced
in Ch.~\ref{ch:theoretical_framework} and~\ref{ch:data_setup}, assessed with the
metrics defined in Ch.~\ref{ch:evaluation_methodology}. Two research questions guide
the analysis:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{RQ1. Phase~1 --- Replication.} Is a CSDI-based diffusion architecture capable
    of learning the distribution and reproducing the stylized facts of financial time series when trained
    under the SDE framework proposed by Kim et al.~\cite{kim2025diffusion}?
    \item \textbf{RQ2. Phase~2 --- EDM-CSDI.} Does embedding the same architecture within the EDM framework of Karras et al.~\cite{karras2022elucidating}
    and conditioning generation on same-day Open, High, and Low log-returns improve
    generation quality over the Phase~1 baseline?
\end{enumerate}

The chapter is organised in four sequential parts. The first part introduces two baseline models, GARCH-X and Merton-X, which serve as a baseline comparison against parametric models that are widely accepted in industry, fully understood, and probabilistically modeled. The second part covers Phase~1, which concerns the replication of the work of Kim et al.~\cite{kim2025diffusion} on unconditional univariate generation of S\&P~500 close log-returns. The third part investigates the contribution of the EDM framework when applied to Kim et al.'s CSDI-based architecture, retaining unconditional information while introducing preconditioning in training. Lastly, the full EDM model is presented, incorporating conditional information within the EDM framework.

Subsequently, other, smaller experiments were carried out to compare the models' behavior under different sampling procedures.

A key design difference that is worth keeping in mind between Phase~1 and Phase~2 concerns the validation split, as described in Sec.\ref{sec:sliding_window_ch4}. In Phase~1, validation windows are drawn randomly from the full sliding-window pool and are partially overlapping with others. In Phase~2, validation windows are strictly held out: 239 windows, corresponding to approximately 1\% of the overall windows and 5\% of the unique rows. These validation windows while less important for unconditional generation, since hidden date ranges can be seen across different windows, they are fundamental for assessing conditional learning.

Moreover, because of the way the data are processed to build windows, the `Reference' values vary slightly according to the specific configuration being analyzed: raw reference, \(L=2048\) windows with \(s=400\), or \(L=512\) windows with \(s=100\).

Unless stated otherwise, all power-law coefficients \(\hat{\alpha}\) are computed using the \texttt{powerlaw} Python package by Alstott et al.~\cite{alstott2014powerlaw} on a subsample of \(300,000\) observations. Similarly, \(D_{KS}\) and \(W_1\) are computed on a subsample that match the minimum number of observations available between the Reference dataset and the generated ones using a fixed seed. For `Validation` this amount to \(611,840\) \footnote{This is the product between the number of validation windows, the sequence length \(L\), and number of samples per window. Specifically, \(512 \cdot 239\cdot5\) independent samples from Reference.}, while for the other cases is the Reference dataset itself, \(12,303,872\)\footnote{Product between the number of training and validation windows for the sequence length \(L\). Specifically, \(512\cdot (23,792+239)\).}. Finally, excess kurtosis (Kur.) is reported relative to the Gaussian.

\section{Phase 1: Replication}
\label{sec:phase1_results}
Phase~1 aims to replicate the generative approach of Kim et al.~\cite{kim2025diffusion} as detailed in the column Phase~1 of Tab.\ \ref{tab:training_setup}. For each model, a dataset of 512 windows of length \(L=2048\) (\(1,048,576\) observations in total) was generated using the probability-flow ODE, Eq.\ \ref{eq:probability_flow}, with 2000 discretization steps. The number of samples was chosen for computational feasibility. Full results are presented in the appendix Sec.\ \ref{sec:app_phase1_results}.

The statistics of Tab.\ \ref{tab:phase1_aggregate} illustrate the ability of the CSDI model to learn the bulk of the FTS distribution. Specifically, for the VE family and VP+Cosine, quantiles between 1\% and 99\% are well reproduced and the standard deviation remains close to the reference; VP+Linear, VP+Exponential, and all GBM configurations instead show substantial deviations in scale (Std up to \(\sim\)13\(\times\) the reference for GBM). Nevertheless, the models still display two limitations: the inability to learn the peculiar pattern of the IQR range of Fig.\ \ref{fig:garch_merton_thinner} and a failure to capture the heavy-tailed distribution of FTS. Indeed, `Kur.' and `Skew.' are underestimated or unstable across all configurations. The extreme `Kur.' of `VP + Linear' (529.8) is driven by a small number of generated windows with extreme values (Min \(=-3.79\), Max \(=5.30\), Tab.\ \ref{tab:phase1_raw_with_reference}), an order of magnitude beyond any other configuration; excess kurtosis is highly sensitive to such outliers, so this statistic should not be read as representative of VP+Linear's typical sample quality.

Most importantly, interpreting the log-returns as cumulative did not yield any useful advantage. On the other hand, for the GBM family the learning appeared rudimentary: the generated values display near-zero excess kurtosis and skewness (Tab.\ \ref{tab:phase1_aggregate}), indicating they are statistically close to Gaussian rather than to the heavy-tailed reference distribution, and casting doubt on the model's ability to discern signal from noise in these configurations.

From Tab.\ \ref{tab:phase1_aggregate}, the VE family achieves the best overall \(D_{KS}\)/\(W_1\) (VE+Exponential), and within VE the linear schedule clearly underperforms its cosine and exponential counterparts. More generally, the linear schedule is never the best-performing choice on \(D_{KS}\)/\(W_1\) in either SDE family, suggesting it is comparatively the least useful noise schedule for this architecture, even though its effect is less pronounced within VP. Regardless, there are still two distributional limitations of the VE approach: first, it undershoots tail reconstruction, as visible in the QQ-plot of Fig.\ \ref{fig:level_marginal_ve_exponantial_2048}. Secondly, it misses the 0\% returns, as shown by the generated ECDF in Fig.\ \ref{fig:level_marginal_ve_exponantial_2048}, cutting through the central vertical jump.

\textbf{Stylized Facts} \quad The estimate of the \(\alpha\) component is consistent with Kim et al., who reported \(\alpha\approx4.35\), while here it was estimated at \(4.37\). An estimate below this benchmark indicates heavier tails in the data, and viceversa.

The core advantage of Phase~1 models over the baselines is their ability to recover the stylized facts of the training windows, especially the leverage effect, even if this can be reproduced only at a lower scale than the reference as visible in Fig.\ \ref{fig:phase1_stylized_grid}.

\textbf{Summary and Selection of Best Phase~1 Configuration} \quad In conclusion, the models appear to work under certain conditions and provide a good basis for further extensions; nevertheless, the GBM approach must be discarded in its current form and it requires further clarification by the authors Kim et al.\ \cite{kim2025diffusion}.

\section{Phase 2: EDM-CSDI}
\label{sec:ch6_edm_csdi_phase2}
This section explores the results regarding the EDM-CSDI framework. The first part evaluates unconditional generation, analysing the generalization of Phase~1 models, and then training another model, EDM+CSDI (Unc.), with the parameters defined in Tab.\ \ref{tab:training_setup} (Phase~2 column), but using the \texttt{unconditional} mask instead of \texttt{predict\_close}, both seen in Sec.\ \ref{sec:masking_strategies_ch3}, and with the number of features equal to \(K=1\). For a fair comparison with Phase~1, the two most promising models, VE+Exponential and VE+Cosine, were retrained with the same settings as in Phase~1 (Tab.\ \ref{tab:training_setup}), but with \(L=512\) and \(s=100\). The second part of the section explores conditional generation.

\textbf{CRPS and Interval Coverage} \quad For this analysis the EDM-CSDI (Con.) was used to generate two datasets. One with 50 samples for each 2,000 windows, while the other only 25 samples. Both with \(L=512\) and \(s=100\). Generating these samples for each of the \(20,000\) windows was computationally unfeasible given the HPC constraints.

As observable in Tab.\ \ref{tab:crps_results}, the EDM-CSDI model achieve a CRPS skill score of 0.7376 (Both, \(N=50\)), meaning a 73.76\% improvement attributable to conditioning signal. This helps reduce the average MAE from approximately one standard deviation in daily log-returns to under a third, while decreasing the breadth of the prediction. This is true both for `Validation' and for combined (`Both') windows. Interestingly, the validation windows has a lower CRPS, which correlated to the lower MAE, suggest less exposure to rare events and extreme market events.

Regarding the interval coverage, Tab.\ \ref{tab:coverage_results}, higher nominal levels are correlated with more overconfident model's predictions (negative bias). Increasing the number of generated samples from 25 to 50 consistently increases empirical coverage at every nominal level, shifting the model from overconfident (negative bias) toward better-calibrated or underconfident (positive bias) predictions; this shift is sufficient to flip the sign of the bias at the 80\% and 90\% levels, while at 95\% and 99\% it narrows but does not eliminate the overconfidence. For financial markets, where coverage of extreme events is important, more samples (which provide broader coverage of extreme events and better CRPS) seem to be a more reasonable choice.

\section{Summary}
This chapter has explored the implementation and journey that led to the EDM-CSDI (Cond.) model and its ability to replicate both the distributional and the stylized-fact properties of S\&P~500 close log-returns.

Regarding RQ1 (Phase~1), the VE and VP families partially reproduce the bulk of the return distribution, quantiles, standard deviation, and the leverage effect at reduced amplitude, while underestimating or destabilising higher moments and undershooting the heavyness of the tails; the GBM family fails outright, generating near-Gaussian samples (Tab.\ \ref{tab:phase1_aggregate}). VE+Exponential achieves the best distributional similarity (\(D_{KS}=0.037\), \(W_1=0.002\)), while VP+Cosine attains the closest tail exponent (\(\hat{\alpha}=4.368\) vs.\ reference \(4.378\)).

Regarding RQ2 (Phase~2), embedding the architecture within the EDM framework and conditioning on same-day Open, High, and Low log-returns yields a substantial improvement over the Phase~1 baseline: the excess-kurtosis gap relative to the reference narrows from a 49\% deficit (EDM-CSDI Unc., 20.0 vs.\ 39.5) to a 1\% deficit (EDM-CSDI Cond., 39.0 vs.\ 39.5), and \(W_1\) improves roughly of five times (0.0029 to 0.0006, Both split; Tab.\ \ref{tab:phase2_edm_conditional_512_100}). Conditioning also yields a CRPS skill score of approximately 0.74 over the climatology baseline and a substantial reduction in MAE (Tab.\ \ref{tab:crps_results}). Calibration remains imperfect: empirical coverage tracks the nominal level reasonably well at moderate confidence levels but the model remains overconfident at the 95\% and 99\% levels (Tab.\ \ref{tab:coverage_results}).

Taken together, these results indicate that the CSDI-based architecture can learn the bulk of the FTS distribution under the SDE framework of Kim et al.\ \cite{kim2025diffusion}, but only the combination of EDM preconditioning and OHL conditioning closes the gap on the higher-order moments and tail behaviour, which are also the most relevant for risk-sensitive analysis and  financial applications.
