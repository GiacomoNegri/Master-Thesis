\chapter{Experiments and Results}
\label{ch:experiments_results}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:exp_introduction}

This chapter presents the experimental evaluation of the two modelling phases introduced
in Ch.~\ref{ch:theoretical_framework} and~\ref{ch:data_setup}, assessed with the
metrics defined in Ch.~\ref{ch:evaluation_methodology}. Two research questions guide
the analysis:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Phase~1 --- Replication.} Is a CSDI-based diffusion architecture capable
    of learning the distribution and reproducing the stylized facts of financial time series when trained
    under the SDE framework proposed by Kim et al.~\cite{kim2025diffusion}?
    \item \textbf{Phase~2 --- EDM-CSDI.} Does embedding the same architecture within the EDM framework of Karras et al.~\cite{karras2022elucidating}
    and conditioning generation on same-day Open, High, and Low log-returns improve
    generation quality over the Phase~1 baseline?
\end{enumerate}

The chapter is organised in two sequential parts: Phase~1 (Sec.~\ref{sec:phase1}) replicates the work of Kim et al.~\cite{kim2025diffusion} on
unconditional univariate generation of S\&P~500 close log-returns.
Phase~2 (Sec.~\ref{sec:phase2}) extends the validated backbone to the multivariate OHLC
setting within the EDM framework, evaluating conditional generation quality on both training windows and fully held-out validation windows.

A key design difference between the two phases concerns the validation split, as described in Sec.\ref{sec:sliding_window_ch4}. In Phase~1,
validation windows are drawn randomly from the full sliding-window pool. In Phase~2, validation windows are strictly held out.

Both phases use the same underlying CSDI-inspired backbone, whose details are available Tab.~\ref{tab:training_setup}. Metric definitions are readable at Ch. ~\ref{ch:evaluation_methodology}.

\subsection{Conditional Generation Quality}
\label{sec:phase2_conditional}

The defining evaluation of Phase~2 is the quality of the conditional predictive
distribution $p(r^C \mid r^O, r^H, r^L)$ generated by the model at each time step
within each window. Unlike the distributional metrics of Sec.~\ref{sec:phase2_comparison},
which assess whether the marginal distribution of generated Close log-returns resembles
the training data, the CRPS and interval coverage metrics assess whether individual
conditional forecasts are accurate and calibrated given the specific OHL context
provided at inference time.

For each window, the model generates an ensemble of Close log-return trajectories,
each conditioned on the same observed OHL channels. The ensemble is used to compute
the CRPS (as defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation_methodology}) and the empirical
interval coverage at nominal levels $\alpha \in \{0.01, 0.05, 0.50, 0.75, 0.99\}$.
Both metrics are computed separately for training windows and for the strictly held-out
validation windows. A large gap between training and validation performance would
indicate overfitting to the temporal patterns seen during training, while comparable
performance across both splits would indicate that the model has learned conditioning
relationships that generalise to unseen market periods.

\subsection{Stylized Facts under Conditional Generation}
\label{sec:phase2_stylized}

A conditional model that achieves strong pointwise CRPS performance may still fail to
reproduce the dynamic statistical properties of the Close log-return series at the
sequence level. This section repeats the stylized fact evaluation of
Sec.~\ref{sec:phase1_stylized} on the generated Close channel from Phase~2 BM, using
the same metrics and the same visual format, so that direct comparison with Phase~1 is
possible without any ambiguity about evaluation protocol. In Phase~2, only the Close
channel is generated; the OHL channels retain their observed values and are not evaluated
as generative outputs. The stylized facts are therefore computed on the marginal
distribution of generated Close log-returns within each window.

Conditioning on OHL should not, in principle, prevent the model from reproducing the
stylized facts of the Close marginal: fat tails, volatility clustering, and the leverage
effect are properties of the underlying data-generating process and are present in the
conditional distribution $p(r^C \mid r^O, r^H, r^L)$ as well as in the unconditional
marginal. However, if the model exploits the OHL conditioning to reduce forecast variance
--- for example, by placing most ensemble mass near the conditional mean --- extreme Close
returns may be suppressed relative to their unconditional frequency. If this occurs, it
constitutes an informative result about the trade-off between conditional accuracy and
marginal distributional fidelity under the current model parametrisation.

\subsection{Unconditional EDM Baseline}
\label{sec:phase2_unconditional}

The comparison between Phase~1$^*$ and Phase~2 BM in Sec.~\ref{sec:phase2_comparison}
conflates two distinct sources of any observed performance difference: the change in
training framework (from the Song et al.~\cite{song2021score} SDE formulation to EDM)
and the introduction of OHLC conditioning information. Without an intermediate model
that applies the EDM framework while withholding the conditioning signal, it is not
possible to attribute any observed improvement to conditioning alone rather than to the
framework change.

To isolate these contributions, an unconditional EDM model is trained using the same
architecture, EDM loss, preconditioning, and noise schedule as Phase~2 BM, but with
$m^{\text{co}} = \mathbf{0}$ everywhere and $K=1$, so that only the univariate Close
log-return channel is provided and no OHL side information is available at any stage of
training or inference. This model is structurally identical to Phase~2 BM in its training
framework and window configuration ($L=512$, $s=100$), and structurally identical to
Phase~1$^*$ in its conditioning (none). Any performance difference between Phase~1$^*$
and the unconditional EDM model can therefore be attributed to the framework change,
while any remaining gap between the unconditional EDM model and Phase~2 BM can be
attributed to the OHL conditioning signal. If the unconditional EDM model matches or
exceeds Phase~2 BM on distributional metrics, this would indicate that the EDM framework
itself is responsible for the bulk of any improvement over Phase~1$^*$, and that the OHL
conditioning contributes primarily to conditional forecast calibration rather than to
marginal distributional fidelity.

\subsection{Sampler Ablations}
\label{sec:phase2_sampler}

A key advantage of the EDM framework is the decoupling of the numerical sampler from
the training objective, enabling inference-only experiments without any retraining. The
following three ablations are applied to the Phase~2 BM checkpoint and, where indicated,
to the Phase~1$^*$ checkpoint, to characterise the sensitivity of generation quality to
sampler configuration. All ablations are conducted after training is complete and involve
only changes to the inference procedure.

\textbf{Stochastic Sampler --- \(S_{\text{churn}}\).}
In its deterministic form, the Heun probability flow ODE produces identical Close
trajectories for identical OHL context across all ensemble members, since the ODE is
fully determined by its initial condition and the learned score field. For conditional
generation, this implies that all ensemble samples collapse to the same trajectory for
a given OHL window, eliminating the ability to quantify predictive uncertainty through
ensemble spread. While this is acceptable for point forecasting, it renders the model
unable to represent the distribution $p(r^C \mid r^O, r^H, r^L)$, which is the primary
evaluation object in Phase~2. The parameter $S_{\text{churn}}$ injects controlled
stochastic perturbations at each denoising step, restoring ensemble diversity by adding
noise at a rate calibrated to the local curvature of the score field. Larger values of
$S_{\text{churn}}$ increase the spread of the generated ensemble but may also introduce
additional discretisation error if the perturbations push trajectories into regions of
the score field where the ODE approximation is less accurate.

\textbf{Prior Sensitivity --- \(\sigma_{\max}\).}
The value of $\sigma_{\max}$ at inference time defines the noise level of the prior
distribution from which sampling begins. A value that is too low means the sampler
starts in a partially denoised state, potentially inheriting residual structure from
the initialisation rather than generating from the correct prior. A value that is too
high wastes denoising budget on noise levels that carry no recoverable information about
the data, and may introduce numerical issues if the score estimate is poorly conditioned
at extreme noise levels. This ablation characterises the sensitivity of Phase~1$^*$ and
Phase~2 BM to the choice of $\sigma_{\max}$, providing practical guidance on the
robustness of the inference procedure.

\textbf{Number of Denoising Steps --- NFEs.}
Karras et al.~\cite{karras2022elucidating} report that the Heun sampler achieves sample
quality comparable to Euler--Maruyama at approximately seven times fewer neural function
evaluations, due to the higher-order truncation error and the concentration of
discretisation steps at low $\sigma$ where ODE curvature is highest
(Sec.~\ref{sec:edm_literature}). For deployment in applications such as scenario
generation or Monte Carlo simulation, sampling efficiency is practically relevant, since
the cost of generation scales linearly with the number of denoising steps. In the Heun
sampler, each denoising step requires two network evaluations (one for the predictor and
one for the corrector), so a schedule of $N$ steps incurs a total of $2N$ NFEs.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:experiments_summary}

This chapter has presented the experimental evaluation of two complementary modelling
phases. Phase~1 assessed seven SDE and noise schedule configurations on unconditional
univariate generation of S\&P~500 close log-returns, seeking to replicate the claims of
Kim et al.~\cite{kim2025diffusion}. Phase~2 embedded the same architecture within the
EDM framework with OHLC conditioning, evaluating both conditional forecast calibration
and marginal distributional fidelity on training and strictly held-out validation windows.
